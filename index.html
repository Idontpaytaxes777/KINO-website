Кино

The underground sound that defined the soviet post-punk.

1. The Underground Beginnings (1981–1983)

In the early 1980s, rock music was strictly controlled and censored in the Soviet Union. In Leningrad (now St. Petersburg), 19-year-old Viktor Tsoi and Aleksei Rybin formed a group initially called Garin i giperboloidy, soon renaming it Kino ("Cinema").

Working secretly within the legendary Leningrad Rock Club, Kino recorded their debut album, 45 (1982), with the help of Boris Grebenshchikov, leader of the established rock band Aquarium. The album had a raw, acoustic acoustic-post-punk sound with romantic, everyday lyrics that immediately resonated with Soviet youth.

2. Finding the Signature Sound (1984–1986)

After internal lineup changes, guitarist Yuri Kasparyan joined the group, helping shape Kino’s iconic music style—driven by crisp guitar riffs, deep bass lines, and drum machines influenced by Western post-punk acts like The Cure and Joy Division.

During this period, Viktor Tsoi famously worked as a stoker in a Leningrad boiler room nicknamed "Kamchatka" to avoid Soviet legal penalties for "social parasitism." The boiler room became an underground hub for artists and musicians.

3. Perestroika & "Changes!" (1987–1988)

As Mikhail Gorbachev introduced the policies of Glasnost (openness) and Perestroika (restructuring), Kino emerged from the underground into mainstream stardom.

In 1987, Kino released the song "Khochu peremen!" ("I Want Changes!"), which became an anthem for youth demanding political and social reform. That same year, Tsoi starred in the iconic cult film Igla ("The Needle"), catapulting him to national fame as a cultural hero.

4. "Blood Type" and Stadium Mania (1988–1990)

The release of the 1988 album Gruppa krovi ("Blood Type") turned "Kinomania" into a full-blown national phenomenon across the USSR and Eastern Europe.

Kino filled massive sports stadiums. On **June 24, 1990**, Kino played their last major concert at Moscow's Luzhniki Stadium before 62,000 cheering fans, featuring the famous Olympic flame lit in their honor.

5. Tragic Loss & Legacy (1990–Present)

On **August 15, 1990**, Viktor Tsoi tragically died in a car crash near Riga, Latvia, at just 28 years old. His sudden death shocked millions across the Soviet Union.

The surviving band members released one final studio effort, known as The Black Album (1990), using recovered vocal tracks Tsoi had recorded shortly before his death.

"Tsoi is alive!" (Цой жив!)

To this day, graffiti reading "Tsoi is alive!" can be found across cities in Eastern Europe, preserving Kino's legacy as a eternal symbol of freedom and youthful spirit.
